## Introduction

Following up on my earlier blog: [The self-fulfilling prophecy](/nl/blog/de-zichzelf-vervullende-voorspelling/), I want to go a little deeper into the invisible costs an agile transformation can bring with it, if it is not properly led and guided from the top. Read that blog first to put this one in context.

Most companies are busy becoming more agile, with agile ways of working as an aid. The observation is that most companies have great difficulty letting go of old ways of working, governance and structures. Not letting go of these leads to invisible extra costs, because old and new continue to exist side by side in conflict. This can add up to a lot per month. We also call this the cost of delay.

(For an explanation of ‘cost of delay’, see appendix 1).

## 6 old shoes

If we look at the hybrid situation as described in the aforementioned blog, there are a number of things that (hopefully temporarily) happen twice (in the traditional and the new way):

1.  Double estimating, budgeting and planning
2.  New working formats on top of existing meeting structures
3.  Double reporting
4.  Double or overlapping roles
5.  Double language
6.  Dual way of governing

From my experience I say that you cannot prevent this, and that it will be like this for six months to a year, until you can throw away the **old shoes**. But then the **new shoes** do have to work! So if you fail to throw away the old shoes, you start incurring costs that would not have been necessary.

(For a calculation example of the cost of delay of not getting rid of these old shoes as soon as possible, see appendix 2. There I calculate that the invisible costs of not getting rid of old shoes in time can approach a million euros for a tribe with 10 teams of 10 employees).

Below a short explanation per shoe:

### Re 1: Double estimating, budgeting and planning

Classic expert hour estimates require that the solution is already known. But we work in an agile environment with the WHAT, and not the HOW, in the backlog. Classic expert estimates therefore enforce waterfall working (design first). A new way of estimating and forecasting needs to be built up through relative estimating and building up frames of reference (history). This takes time (6 months to 1 year with good guidance). In the absence of that frame of reference, the existing way of budgeting cannot be done away with yet.

### Re 2: New working formats on top of existing meeting structures

New rhythmic working formats are carried out multidisciplinarily. This makes many existing meeting structures redundant. However, these new working formats are often experienced as extra pressure because the existing meeting structures have not yet gone. Management should actively steer (together with the teams) on eliminating as many existing meeting structures as possible.

### Re 3: Double reporting

Drawing up reports takes a lot of time. Making timely decisions based on current information is actually made harder by the lead times of reporting lines. Current and visual metrics are kept up to date by teams. These make the so-called ‘gemba walk’ and performance dialogue by management on the work floor possible. Management can then make decisions and remove impediments on the basis of much more current information, with background information straight from the source. So immediately investigate which existing reporting lines can go, and get rid of them too.

### Re 4: Double or overlapping roles

Organizations often have several structural dimensions, each with its own management, coordinating roles and meeting structures (departments, programmes, projects, agile teams). So it is very likely that employees in one team have to answer to several managers. That is very undesirable and creates entanglement of interests.

Turn tribes into departments and, if possible, limit yourself to 1 structural dimension (e.g. Tribe). Reduce the number of double or overlapping management and coordination roles

### Re 5: Double language

In a transition situation old and new language are often used interchangeably. There is also often a lack of a clear new conceptual framework and values. This causes a Babylonian confusion of tongues, and people then discuss process more than content. Create a shared conceptual framework as quickly as possible and ‘stick to it!’. That requires a great effort in training and communication.

### Re 6: Dual way of governing

The existing governance is often still based on start/stop project-based working and bringing people to the work (PUSH). The new way of working is based on product-based working and bringing work to the product teams in a continuous flow of customer needs (PULL).

Letting these two ways of governing exist side by side leads to conflicts and loss of productivity. Product management and project management are at each other's throats on a daily basis. Or even worse, Scrum Masters are held accountable for an obligation to deliver results and treated as project managers. In my experience this has often led to the departure of good employees with a GROW mindset. So phase out the existing way of governing as quickly as possible and give Product management full mandate.

## Back to the ‘Self-fulfilling prophecy’

As said, the monthly cost of delay is invisible to the organization. To the extent that it is usually noticed that productivity has decreased. And then the danger lurks again that the agile transition gets the blame. Often, for lack of knowledge, cuts are then made on precisely the things that could help push the transition through firmly.

## What can be done?

I started the blog by saying that leadership and guidance from the top of the organization is key to having insight into these things, monitoring them and steering on getting rid of those old shoes. That requires a top management that immerses itself in how agile organizations are governed in a totally different way, and that the change towards it needs to be approached holistically.

It should also be noted that, besides saving on the redundant things during a transition period, there are more things that can favourably influence productivity. This on top of the benefits mentioned here for those redundant things. Below a non-exhaustive list:

- Steering on balanced team compositions
- Steering on continuous improvement
- Steering on continuous learning and knowledge sharing through training and communities
- Steering on reducing technical debt
- better focus (so promoting good flow through kanban and pull mechanisms)
- eliminating push behaviour

For anyone who wants advice in this area, or wants to present his or her case to me, I am available. I wish all organizations running this marathon every success and beautiful new shoes!

## Appendix 1: What is ‘Cost of Delay’

Normally ‘Cost of Delay’ is used to determine the value you miss out on (per period) if you put a new piece of service into use later. This can be the revenue you miss out on, but also a risk you do not eliminate as a result, reputational damage, or a mandatory date that, if you do not meet it, results in fines. It is used to prioritize work in an agile environment. Because in an agile organization you want to deliver value in cycles as short as possible. The consideration of ‘what gives us the most value first if we put it into use’ therefore plays a major role.

So here it is used in operations, to innovate and maintain your services or products as optimally as possible. But you can also apply the principle to reorganizations, or the form that is common now, agile transformations.

## Appendix 2: Calculation example of the cost of delay

A tribe with 10 teams of 10 employees has been started, but still sits on top of the existing organization, which has not yet moved along. The internal rate of the employees is 75 euros per hour.

Costs re 1: ‘Double estimating, budgeting and planning’. Planning in the new situation takes 4 hours per two weeks. This is more effective than the existing way of expert estimates for everything and anything. Because in the old situation there is no prioritization, so everything is estimated. And the solution must already be known before an expert estimate can be made. So that is even more effort. I cautiously state that estimating, budgeting and planning in the classic situation takes three times as much time, which you can therefore save after six months to a year. 12 hours per 2 weeks.

That results in costs of €180,000 per month, which you can drop after six months to a year.

For all assumptions I make in this blog, it holds that they will differ per organization, and stage of transition. I think I have even been cautious here. But it is always substantial!
