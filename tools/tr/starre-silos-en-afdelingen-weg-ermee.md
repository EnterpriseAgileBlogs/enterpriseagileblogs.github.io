TITLE: Rigid silos and departments: Away with them!
![OrgNext.jpg](/images/blog/OrgNext.jpg)

## Intro

The deeply rooted habits and ways of governing in Anglo-Saxon business make the transition to agile organizations very difficult.

Think of the simple fact that there are still so many departments with names like ‘IT’, ‘IV’ (information provisioning), ‘IM’ (information management) etc. But also staff departments. These are rigid departments that stand apart from the business departments, which often actually bear the names of the products or services a company delivers.

That classic separation alone creates an enormous gap between the business and IT. And the moment Scrum teams are used within the ‘IT’ department, people think the organization is agile and nimble. This is far from true, and is even referred to as ‘Zombie Scrum’ by agile experts.

## What are the impediments?

Classic departments have a department manager, who in turn has departmental plans (usually annual plans), departmental goals etc. etc. In itself it is good to translate company goals into departmental goals, but for the silos mentioned, such as ‘Business’, ‘IT’, ‘IV’ and ‘IM’, it remains sub-optimal. Why:

- Departmental plans often have an annual cycle; that is not agile
- Departmental plans are often followed too strictly, without taking changing circumstances into account. This means managers are often forced to use strange constructions.
- Departmental KPIs are often used that say nothing about how they contribute to the short-cycle delivery of business value, which is what everyone should be focusing on in an agile organization.
- Departmental plans often contain guidelines or requirements for concrete products to be delivered; that should be left to product management, otherwise you get entanglement of interests
- Departments have their own departmental meeting structures, which overlap with or are even redundant to all the agile working formats. So they get in each other's way.
- Departments therefore have their own goals (derived from the company strategy, if you are lucky) that do not necessarily have to match the goals the business has.
- Departments, such as staff departments, often represent one-sided disciplines such as Architecture, HR, Legal, Compliance, Risk. While in an agile organization we want to work with multidisciplinary teams.
- Managers of departments that only cover part of a product chain are often made responsible for the timely delivery of releases for products. People want to be able to blame someone. But in an agile organization this also belongs with product management.
- In different departments that together cover a product chain, different cultures, misunderstanding and communication gaps can arise.
- Departments such as ‘IT’ and ‘IV’ are often far removed from the end customer. And they often have efficiency targets, whereas they should be steering on effectiveness. If a Scrum team asks whether they can go to the customer for a day, they are often even told: ‘That is not possible, because that costs us a whole day of production’.
- Agile teams can have team members with different line managers from different departments, which creates entanglement of interests.

I could certainly think of a few more. But these points give a good picture of what I mean. These points absolutely do NOT contribute to becoming or being an agile organization. These are symptoms of a rigid, hierarchical, Anglo-Saxon organization.

## How then?

In agile transitions you see other structures. The basic structure or ‘building block’ is a multidisciplinary team. Then you see teams of teams. Often called ‘Tribes’, ‘Release Trains’, ‘Clusters’, ‘Grids’, ‘Nexus’. These teams of teams are oriented on a product or service and, if all is well, contain teams that include both the business and the IT discipline. In this blog: [Culture follows structure](/nl/blog/cultuur-volgt-structuur/) I go even further and even see multidisciplinary staff teams being part of these teams of teams.

Can you then call such a team of teams a department from now on? Yes and no.

**Why no?**

Departments are often static in nature, have departmental plans etc. as described above. In the classic sense it is therefore not a department, because these teams of teams, with their product management, stand together as business and IT for 1 product or service. They have no plans, but a product vision and roadmaps with a rolling forecast. They have no reports, but Obeya rooms where the current state of affairs is discussed every week in a fixed rhythm. Things for which separate projects are normally announced in a departmental plan, such as upgrading platforms, improving contract management etc., are now included as enabler epics in the backlog and roadmaps.

**Why yes?**

In an organizational sense, and to make lean budgeting possible, it can be handy to label a team of teams as a department. Especially in the ERP system. Because, after all, in lean budgeting the (annual, adjustable per quarter) personnel costs of a team of teams are also the product budgets! How handy it can then be to place the employees of one team of teams in your system in one department for financial management.

But that may be where a bottleneck lies. In my experience most ERP systems, together with the managerial processes around them, are so rigid that it can take weeks of approvals, and processes across departments, before an employee can be moved from one department to another. But in an agile organization it should be super easy (I can already picture an ERP system where you can move teams with drag and drop…😉) to make flexible adjustments with it (if you need an ERP system at all). See my blog: [the agile organization according to the Boston matrix](/nl/blog/de-wendbare-organisatie-analoog-de-boston-matrix/), where I show that it must be possible to do this with ease every quarter.

But it is not that simple. ERP systems are filled with job classification systems (see my blog: [Job classification systems: demolish them!](/nl/blog/functiehuizen-slopen/) ) and structures and business rules. So all of that must become simpler and more flexible too. These are things that the staff departments HR and Control (yes, multidisciplinary!) need to think about together.

Who knows of an ERP system that is that dynamic? Or is it precisely the organization structures and rules that make an ERP system rigid, and are most ERP systems in principle suitable for it?

## Epilogue

For a moment it seems to be about ERP systems. But no, the main theme is agile organizations. And that we cannot become an agile organization if we keep holding on to rigid org charts that may only change once every 5 years in a big reorganization. And with that it is about flexibility and adaptability of organization structure, governance, management, employees, and the underlying processes and tools that support the organization internally.

All of this can of course be nuanced. Because how far should you go in this? What type of organization are you, how innovative do you need to be as an organization, and so on. But commercial companies in particular will not be able to escape this dance.
