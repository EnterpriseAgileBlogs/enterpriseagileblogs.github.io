## Introduction

In my blog about ‘Development stage 4: DevOps within IT’ I explained the pros and cons of merging the development and operations departments into so-called domain departments. Insofar as this step is opportune, it will again yield more return than when operations and development are structured separately.

In this last part we will look in particular at the everlasting movement between centralization and decentralization of IT, and the drivers behind it. A new driver for decentralizing now seems to be creating the ultimate agile organization. A decentralized organization would after all be able to respond faster to, or even anticipate, changes in its environment. But will that really work? Even though I do not yet know of any organization that is organized this way, I will make an attempt to substantiate why this could be a good final step in creating an agile organization.

In this blog I bring in the topics of sociotechnics and holacracy.

For reference, here are the development stages once more:

1.  Working iteratively within projects in the matrix
2.  Virtual product teams across functional departments
3.  Physical product teams, whether or not virtually supplemented with Ops
4.  DevOps within IT
5.  Enterprise-wide product or domain departments

This is the 5<sup>th</sup> blog in a series of 5, about the 5<sup>th</sup> agile maturity step.

## Sociotechnics, the fifties revived!

From Wikipedia:

*According to Frans van Eijnatten, sociotechnics is an "applied science that strives to improve the functioning of both people and organization by adapting or fundamentally redesigning work processes and the organization of technology or services, as well as human work tasks"*

*Ulbo de Sitter is the founder of sociotechnics in the Netherlands and summarizes the core of sociotechnics as: "complex tasks in a simple organization instead of simple tasks in a complex organization". This in relation to a need present at that time for more decisiveness of the organization towards the sales market and service provision on the one hand. And on the other hand, to achieve a better fit with the labour market. Better and more highly educated employees were looking for more interesting jobs than were available at that time.*

## Application

*Important contributions from sociotechnics to the design of organizations are the concepts of:*

- **whole task groups** *(later: self-organizing teams!);*
- **flow-oriented production** (*read: by business domain, value streams!);*
- **production structure and control structure** *(Work is product-oriented instead of project-based; governance is adapted accordingly);*
- **integral organizational renewal, which in the Netherlands originated with the NKWO Foundation and the KOERS advisory group** *(see also holacracy);*
- **quality of the organization, quality of work and quality of labour relations** *(see also the intrinsic motivators of Dan Pink).*

Agile working in ‘Value Streams’, you say? Apparently, shortly after the Second World War there was already a movement in the Netherlands that promoted making intelligent work attractive over mass production. In the years that followed, companies went back to embracing traditional efficiency thinking (software development as assembly line work). This as opposed to the interaction between people and organization, and in particular the flexible deployment of people and organization. Apparently simplifying the organization was already promoted back then, and that is exactly what I also want to promote in this last part of the blog series.

## The everlasting wave motion of centralization and decentralization of the IT and business organization structure versus the architecture (IT landscape)

You can apply centralization and decentralization to several aspects. In this blog we look at the organization structure (simplifying along value streams), and the IT landscape.

We still remember that IT originated within the business departments because employees produced all kinds of Excel-like applications to lighten their own tasks. In the long run this became unmaintainable, there was no documentation of it, and it was error-prone. That was one of the reasons to centralize the IT landscape, besides the cost aspect. Total cost of ownership drove architects to propose the same application for all business domains. This led to horizontalization of the IT landscape, whereas the business domains are vertical to it.

![Figure 1. Value streams for business products are perpendicular to the horizontal IT landscape](/images/blog/waardestromen.png)

*Figure 1. Value streams for business products are perpendicular to the horizontal IT landscape*

In his article in XR magazine, [Centralisatie of decentralisatie - wanneer houdt dit nu eens op?](http://www.xr-magazine.nl/artikelen/229/strategie-beleid/centralisatie-decentralisatie-wanneer-houdt-dit-nu-eens-op) (Centralization or decentralization – when will it ever stop?), Mark Paauwe states that the wave motion will never stop. He also says that IT trends such as SOA, web services and cloud make the drivers for centralizing the IT landscape disappear. Therefore this should no longer be an obstacle to decentralizing the organization structure for innovation and maintenance per business domain or value stream. In practice, however, this has many snags. See the pros and cons below.

For realizing an ultimate agile enterprise I now see the trend towards decentralization of the organization structure based on business domains or value streams. This versus the already centralized IT landscape which, through increased flexibility and reduction of technical debt, will have to support the aforementioned decentralization of the organization structure.

## Holacracy: the ultimate form of continuous improvement!

This new form of governing an organization assumes that every employee is a sensor for problems, waste and opportunities. By granting employees or teams autonomy and a mandate to improve processes and even the organization structure, within set boundaries, actors are created who can actually implement improvements. All of this does serve a certain purpose, a business purpose. This fits perfectly with creating autonomous self-organizing teams, lowering content-related decision-making in the organization, continuous process improvement and value creation as the main goal of agile thinking. It is, however, a radically different way of running a business, one that makes many layers of management redundant, or even makes the entire management in the traditional sense redundant.

Why not continuously adapt the roles in the organization to the new roles that emerge from a different approach being chosen? When choosing an agile framework, as is now being done on a large scale, you will see roles such as Scrum masters and Product Managers and Owners emerge.

And why not simplify the structure of the organization and focus it on the value streams of the business products one wants to deliver to the end customer?

Just as you, as a mammoth tanker, need to revise the composition of fixed teams to the increase or decrease of innovation work (what is the trend of your portfolio backlog), you also need to regularly review the organization structure against the status of the life cycle (Star, Cash Cow, Problem child?) of your business products.

The agile principle ‘At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, then tunes and adjusts its behavior accordingly’, which stands for continuous process improvement, could therefore also be projected onto the organization. Continuous organizational improvement is what holacracy stands for. It seems to be the ultimate agile movement at the organizational level. I opt for continuous improvement throughout the whole company. From self-improvement by a team every sprint, to improvement of the structure by management, for example once every six months, driven by the impulses of the employees, who serve as the sensors for this!

## Pros and cons of development stage 5:

Pros:

- The business domain has its own delivery and support teams, who have knowledge of the domain.
- The organization is able to adapt itself to what the business demands in terms of innovation and development, or divestment, of business products.
- Each business domain can set its own requirements for the release trains that support innovation and maintenance of their business products, think of focus on time to market versus quality.
- Management has only 1 goal: optimizing value creation within the domain. No more derived goals per department / island.

Cons:

- Requires a healthy balance between flexibility of the IT landscape and the degree of technical and functional debt (a healthy IT capability), which is far from present at most companies.
- Requires working with feature teams, which in turn means a shift in configuration management, raises HR issues, and requires the aforementioned healthy IT capability.
- Requires a management that also thinks and acts completely agile, and practices continuous organizational improvement based on the product life cycle status.
- Complexity and size of departments (span of control) and the IT landscape (many interfaces) can make simplifying the structure and making the IT landscape more flexible difficult to virtually impossible.
- Managers must be able, and be empowered, to make themselves redundant, while management is precisely aimed at ‘managing’ and therefore maintaining a status quo.

In terms of return percentage this step gets 200%. With thanks to colleague Jasper Doornbos for reviewing and adding to it.
