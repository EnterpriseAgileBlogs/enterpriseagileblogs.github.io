## **Introduction**

In my experience with setting up agile ways of working across organizations, I have always been intrigued by the structure of an organization. And by this I do not just mean the org chart, but also all the informal structures present in an organization, and the hierarchy and roles defined in the organization.

The most important paradigm shifts are the shifts from inside-out to outside-in thinking, from functional to product-oriented structuring, and from an Anglo-Saxon to a Rhineland way of leading and organizing. Tilting organizations from an Anglo-Saxon to a Rhineland organization structure and governance is actually what implicitly needs to happen in agile transitions.

![Figure 1: tilting organizations from Anglo-Saxon to Rhineland oriented](/images/blog/fractal1.png)

*Figure 1: tilting organizations from Anglo-Saxon to Rhineland oriented*

*(See the important work of Jaap Peters on this subject:* [*https://vimeo.com/48072510*](https://vimeo.com/48072510) * *

What you see in the figure on the right are self-organizing teams of self-organizing multidisciplinary teams. This means that business owners, IT, architecture, UX and possibly staff departments such as legal, risk, compliance, marketing and sales are all included in those product-oriented teams of teams. These product-oriented teams of teams are also referred to as Value Streams.

As Jaap Peters says in the video, because those teams have oriented themselves on the product, they are able to place the decisions they make in the context of their product (and therefore the end customer). In this way it contributes to the quality and acceptance of the product. The quality of the product is also determined by the amount of technical, functional and UX debt in the IT landscape. Those teams therefore take more account of reducing that debt than an organization in which functions and (sub-optimal) responsibilities are spread over different (functional) departments.

This orientation requires only one (business) manager leading a team of teams. So there is no need for a separate IT manager or IT department.

But there is much more to say about it.

## The fractal structure pattern in large organizations

## 1. Team level

I see the following fractal structure pattern emerging when scaling product-oriented teams of teams.

A single team is (already according to Scrum) business-oriented. We call that a feature team. However, many classically oriented organizations start with teams oriented on one (or more) application(s), which therefore do not represent business value. We call those teams component teams, because on their own they are not able to realize business value. So a mix of business-oriented and component-oriented teams can emerge.

This looks as follows:

![Figure 2: Feature teams in green, component team in orange](/images/blog/fractal2.png)

*Figure 2: Feature teams in green, component team in orange*

The backlog of a feature team consists of User Stories for one specific business product. The backlog of a component team consists of User Stories requested by both feature teams. Example: feature team 1 is called Collections and feature team 2 is called Payments. The component team can be a support team or mid-tier team that supports both feature teams. Of course, the ideal situation would be a flat organization consisting of nothing more than feature teams and a CEO. That would look like this:

![Figure 3: An ideal flat organization consisting only of feature teams](/images/blog/fractal3.png)

*Figure 3: An ideal flat organization consisting only of feature teams*

In practice, however, you almost always also have component teams. In any case, it depends on many factors whether an organization decides for component or feature teams or not. The degree of ‘loose coupling’ (read: flexibility) of your IT landscape is, among others, an important factor here. So a combination of component and feature teams is neither forbidden nor strange.

## 2. Team of teams level

When we have more than 5 teams, or 50 employees, oriented on one product, we call that a team of teams, or a tribe. It means these people all work together in the same cadence and flow to innovate and maintain a product. Schematically it would look like this:

![Figure 4: A tribe consisting of 2 feature teams and one component team](/images/blog/fractal4.png)

*Figure 4: A tribe consisting of 2 feature teams and one component team*

Note: this is just schematic, to emphasize a fractal character. I already said you should not organize into a tribe if fewer than 50 people are involved. Then you can stay at team level.

## 3. Value stream level

If a product can be innovated and maintained by between 50 and 150 people, or about 5 to 15 teams, you can make do with just one tribe. However, if a product requires more than 150 people or 15 teams to innovate and maintain, you can organize into a value stream for that product with multiple tribes. Tribes in turn can again be business- or component-oriented. This would look as follows:

![Figure 5: A value stream made up of 3 tribes](/images/blog/fractal5.png)

*Figure 5: A value stream made up of 3 tribes*

If we then also visualize the position of the teams within the tribes (again, I keep the pattern of one component team with 2 feature teams for schematic reasons; in reality you would of course see all kinds of different compositions), it would look like this:

![Figure 6: A value stream made up of one component-oriented tribe and two business-oriented tribes, each again consisting of one component team and two feature teams](/images/blog/fractal6.png)

*Figure 6: A value stream made up of one component-oriented tribe and two business-oriented tribes, each again consisting of one component team and two feature teams*

## 4. Enterprise level

Now take a telco. As an example, it has these two value streams:

- Telephony
- Television

So the pattern can even be extended to the enterprise-wide level. This looks as follows:

![Figure 7: A company with two business value streams (products) and one component or shared-service oriented value stream.](/images/blog/fractal7.png)

*Figure 7: A company with two business value streams (products) and one component or shared-service oriented value stream.*

The shared service center could, for example, represent the infrastructure services. The backlog of this shared service center will largely be filled by requests from the two business-oriented value streams.

If we again assume that these are very large value streams with more than 15 teams each, they can in turn contain multiple tribes. This completes the fractal character and looks as follows:

![Figure 8: The enterprise-wide view of the (theoretical) fractal structure](/images/blog/fractal8.png)

*Figure 8: The enterprise-wide view of the (theoretical) fractal structure*

Again, you will never find this pure fractal character in practice. But what I hope to emphasize is that there is a totally new way of looking at the structure of companies. It is product-oriented and can consist of more layers, depending on the scale and size of the company involved. And a truly agile organization will look every quarter at whether this structure still works given the popularity (and thus the employees needed for innovation and maintenance) of their product portfolio. There you have the organization that continuously adapts to circumstances, the agile organization. For another view on this agility, see also my blog (Boston).

And again, I emphasize that the goal should always be to keep the organization as flat as possible. But I know from experience that this is not always possible in large organizations.
