TITLE: Job classification systems? Demolish them!
![sloopkogel.png](/images/blog/sloopkogel.png)

I come across it more and more, and it has been going on for quite a while. Employees who consciously or unconsciously deviate from the tasks as described in their job profile in the job classification system. New ways of working, roles, T-profiles, more ownership, self-organization, continuous improvement, continuous learning: all these things cause employees' tasks to deviate and shift more and more (and keep doing so) relative to their job profiles. What is HR doing about it?

## **Examples:**

Some concrete examples from my practice:

An employee in healthcare who has patient care activities in his job profile is given more and more responsibility for setting up and managing a department over a period of 8 years. During those years the tasks performed involving direct contact with patients decrease to zero, and the coordinating tasks increase more and more. Eventually budget cuts have to be made, and this employee is told to go back to performing patient care activities. The employee's ambitions, however, have already arrived at a completely different level. No one looks at the employee's performance and ambitions, only at the numbers. The employee becomes demotivated.

A designer at a government institution is selected for the role of Product Owner. She performs this work for several years to everyone's full satisfaction. When her manager changes, she is put back in her original position, again to the great demotivation of the employee. The government job classification system contains no position for Product Owner.

An architect at a commercial company performs engineering work in a team for 1 year. During his annual evaluation this employee is rated poorly, because he has not performed the tasks as stated in his job profile.

These are just 3 examples of how you can demotivate employees to the bone by holding on to petty rules and things that are not even current anymore. Organizations are increasingly in motion; reorganizations are the order of the day. And, in line with my blog series ‘From Scrum team to agile organization’, at the highest maturity level an agile organization even practises continuous organizational improvement.

## **Employee motivation leads to better performance**

My experience, but also scientifically proven, is that motivated employees deliver better results and are willing to do much more than when they are demotivated (Gosh, stating the obvious, so why does no one seem to get it?). 3 intrinsic motivators play a role in this:

1.  Autonomy: the (degree of) self-determination an employee (a professional with expertise) is given within the boundaries the organization sets.
2.  Purpose and connection: the degree to which the employee is involved in the goals, as well as in the decision-making to move in the direction of the organization's goals (content decisions as low as possible in the organization).
3.  Mastery: the degree to which employees are enabled to improve themselves and their team, and thus the organization.

There are many ways to demotivate an employee. But the examples mentioned I find truly painful. The fact that an employee has deviated from the tasks as described in his/her position usually serves the goals of the organization, or their organizational unit. So motivator 2 is nullified when someone is kicked back into his/her ‘pen’.

The fact that moving along with the organization is punished in this way means that motivator 3 is also completely nullified. Improving yourself for the benefit of the organization's results is punished.

The degree of self-determination (deciding for yourself which tasks contribute best to the goals of your organization (unit) within the boundaries given to you) is also nullified. Via a command and control structure you are told to go back into your ‘pen’.

I cannot stress enough how destructive this is for an organization.

## **What then?**

The title of this blog is of course exaggerated; I like to do that to make a point. And fortunately there is a whole movement going on that is trying to get the HR function to move along with the growth towards agile organizations too.

If you do want to keep using job classification systems, then at least adapt them in short cycles to the current situation and needs of the organization. For example once per quarter. I also advise all works councils that sometimes dig in their heels on adapting job classification systems to stimulate this movement instead of blocking it. After all, you also stand for the satisfaction and health of the employee, don't you?

But in the extreme case you no longer have a job classification system. Ricardo Semler, owner of pump manufacturer Semco, once gave his employees these boundaries: ‘This is the profit margin I want, this is the part of the revenue that may be spent on salaries, now divide the pie’ (Revenue went through the roof….).

We also see a movement of all staff functions towards the product chains. So, if a team of teams is responsible for a certain product, there is also a supporting team at product or domain level with a domain HR employee, a domain architect, a domain controller, a domain security officer, a domain risk officer, etc. etc. In this way you could even let the arrangements for these areas of attention differ in detail per product line (main boundaries at company level, more detailed boundaries at domain level)

In any case, the domain staff officers (insofar as these need to be single physical employees) are much more involved in the movement and the vision of a particular product line or domain. They also have only one goal: contributing to the short-cycle delivery of customer value by the team of teams they belong to. So I see no merit at all in a staff officer for a functional silo. He or she puts the interest of the functional silo first, which is sub-optimal, and therefore not the interest of a product his/her company delivers.

At enterprise level you then retain only 1 enterprise employee per staff function in a supporting office, who form a community with their peers in the domain departments, and in that way ensure that the main principles for the entire organization also remain safeguarded.

## **The Agile HR manifesto**

Finally, I want to point to 1 of the many initiatives in the HR area, and I want to encourage it. This is only about the HR function. But as you notice, I see all staff functions acting differently (more locally, more flexibly) in the future. I have many examples of this too. I will leave it at HR as an example, and point to the Agile HR Manifesto as drawn up by Agile HR practitioners worldwide ([**https://www.agilehrmanifesto.org/**](https://www.agilehrmanifesto.org/)).

In case the link does not work: here is the manifesto that, as far as I am concerned, should apply to everyone in an organization, but should especially be stimulated and practised by leaders and HR officers:

*We are uncovering better ways of developing an engaging workplace culture by doing it and helping others do it. Through this work we have come to value:*

- ***Collaborative networks**** over hierarchical structures;*
- ***Transparency**** over secrecy;*
- ***Adaptability**** over prescriptive rules;*
- ***Inspiration and engagement**** over management and retention;*
- ***Intrinsic motivation**** over extrinsic rewards;*
- ***Ambition ****over obligation.*

*While there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left (in bold) more.*

*For Agile HR development the following five principles apply:*

1.  *Support employees to feel connected, to grow and to be happy in their workplace.*
2.  *Encourage people to embrace change and adapt when needed.*
3.  *Help build and support networks of empowered, self-organizing and collaborating teams.*
4.  *Stimulate the motivation and skills of employees and the team, help them develop the environment they need and trust them to get the job done.*
5.  *Facilitate and stimulate personal development, to make use of the employee's different strengths and talents.*
