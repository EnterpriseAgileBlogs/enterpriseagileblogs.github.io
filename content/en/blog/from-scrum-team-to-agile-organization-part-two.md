**Agile Maturity step 2: Working in virtual product teams, assembled from functional oriented departments**

In my blog about ‘Agile maturity step 1: Working iteratively within (temporary) projects in a matrix organization’ I explained the advantages and disadvantages of the limited application of working iteratively within projects.

Here the complete existing organization is maintained, such as the matrix organization and project thinking, and will only deliver sub optimal results, if an organization chooses not to go beyond that ambition level.

In this second blog, we go one step further, and create fixed virtual product teams, assembled from the existing functional departments. This step brings its own set of advantages and disadvantages.

Also in this blog, I ‘ll go into the relationship with the fluency model of Shore and Larsen, and I will point out the limitations of this blog series referring to agile implementations against the 7S model of Peters and Waterman.

Below for reference the maturity steps one more time in a row :  
1 .Working iteratively within (temporary) projects in a matrix organization  
2. Working in virtual product teams, assembled from functional oriented departments  
3. Working in physical product teams , possibly supplemented by virtual Ops  
4. DevOps within IT  
5 . Company-wide product or domain teams

**The 1st step of maturity is good for cultivating a proper team culture**  
The fluency model of James Shore and Diana Larsen (See Figure 1.) shows a different view on working towards a complete agile organization. You could say that working iteratively in temporary project teams is similar to the ‘Start Building Code’ of this model , which leads to a ‘Team culture shift’.

With step 2 we want to work on ‘Focus on value’, by assembling virtual product teams. This should lead to a ‘Skill shift’ for those teams.

Skills that fixed product teams can acquire over temporary project teams are:

1. Become predictable at product release level
2. Build on a reference for predictability and on domain knowledge
3. Continuously improve its own processes and thus improve productivity
4. More focus on product quality than on project results (shared code ownership)
5. (Business) product oriented thinking

**Agile maturity step 2 :Working in virtual product teams, assembled from functional oriented departments**

**![Step 2](/images/blog/Stap-2.png)

Figure 2 : Product teams assembled from functional departments

Virtual fixed product teams are assembled from the functional departments. These  (business) product teams (either component or feature teams), are responsible for the innovation as-well as the maintenance of a business product or technical product (component) ‘front to back’.  
In practice, it is quite difficult to distinguish business products. Main business products is often doable, like with an insurance company the main products are: Pensions , Damage and Life insurance. As soon as you need to subdivide these main products, you get into complex discussions.  
These discussions get even further complicated because it is often difficult to put a dedicated team in a chain of applications to cover a sub product. Teams located in the ‘front-‘ , ‘mid-‘ and ‘back-offices’ are often responsible for an entire instance of an application that covers all business products. Here the configuration management and resource issues come up, which replaces the problems of the project silo.  
Still, it’s worth the trouble to try to create fixed teams. Working in fixed product -oriented teams gives employees lots of intrinsic motivation. The product team is self-organizing and directing (Autonomy), working on continuous process improvement (Mastery), and it works on the continuous improvement,  innovation and maintenance of a specific component or (sub) business product (Purpose). See the following video of Daniel Pink on YouTube about these scientifically proved motivators:

**The limitation of the maturity steps discussed**

![seven-s-model](/images/blog/7s.png)

Figure 3 : The 7S Model ( Peters and Waterman, 1979)

The maturity steps in these series are based on the change (whether or not virtual) of the structure of the organization. To properly implement an agile framework, you have to look at far more aspects within an organization. If we look at the seven S model of Peters and Waterman, we should at least also consider the other six areas. In particular, leadership style , competency, training and coaching ( skills) and architecture, governance and budgeting ( systems ) need change also. But you can also think about HR changes like changing function or role descriptions (Staff). The culture (shared values​​) will change along with the changes implemented in the other areas.

**Advantages and disadvantages of agile maturity step 2:**  
**Advantages:**

1. Fixed product teams are a breeding ground for intrinsic motivation.
2. A fixed product team can build on domain knowledge.
3. A fixed product team can build on a reference for predictability.
4. A fixed product team can continuously (rather than temporarily for a project ) work on process and productivity improvement.
5. A fixed product team will feel responsible for the overall quality of a product, not only for the scope of a temporary project (The whip of a project manager in a temporary project team leads to taking short cuts on quality, and thus, to increase of technical debt in IT landscapes. This is a trend, and the amount of technical debt in most large organizations has grown to irreversible proportions).
6. No repetition of actions every time a project for innovation on a specific product is initiated (Staffing, making plans, getting environment up and running, initiating a steering committee and so on).
7. Test environments are permanently kept in the air versus letting them be broken down and built up per project.

**Disadvantages:**

1. Everyone in the team has several bosses ( line manager, product team manager, architect, Product Owner), so there may be conflict of interests.
2. In a pilot with a couple of virtual product teams, resourcing will be a problem. Specialists are often scarce and management is not easily inclined to hire additional staff. What they have to realize is that, when scaling up, many indirect coordination staff can be released, leaving room for hiring more direct specialist staff.
3. Configuration management issues will occur when multiple product teams in the same application layer have to implement features for ‘their’ business product.
4. For large IT landscapes issues will arise about the creation of component or feature teams, and coordination of the synchronization of content of the backlogs for those teams has to be solved.

Some organizations I worked with are in this stage of their agile maturity, including an airline, financial institution and a technical product supplier. It may be a choice of management to not go beyond this step 2 with their agile ambition. But is management making these choices consciously??  This step provides more return on investment already than stage 1, but more can be established when even taking steps 3 and 4.  
**In terms of percentage of return on investment, this step gets a 20%**.
