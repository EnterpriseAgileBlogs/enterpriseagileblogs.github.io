**Because in Agile we focus on the customer requirements, and we have to work disciplined to properly execute the iterations or sprints, and we have to repeat things more often, the perception is that doing things the Agile way costs more time and money. This is a fairy tale.**

**There are two major arguments that say working agile will reduce cost and duration, and increase quality.**

**Argument why defining good requirements will contribute to reduce cost and duration and increase quality:** According to the Butlers and Gartners in this world, the lack of, or inadequate requirements, have been the number one cause for IT projects having overrun for 30 years now!

When working Agile, you have to invest like 20% of your project budget in defining good requirements, something that rarely happens. If people talk about requirements, they often talk about design. And that is where IT projects used to start with. But design is like the answer, as where requirements are the question, or requirements are the problem, as where design describes the solution to the problem.

This ‘extra’ investment is often experienced as additional cost and time, but this 20% extra effort, will be earned back with 40% reduction in cost and duration in the end.

**Argument why working iteratively and risk-driven will contribute to reduce cost and duration**

The law of Boehm dates from 1981 and reads as follows: ‘The  
later in the development process errors are detected, the exponentially higher the repair costs will be’

![](/images/blog/boehms-law-en.png)

The risk-driven approach, where you try to mitigate the biggest risks or impediments first, ensures that you will save exponentially on costs.

According to the experience of the team the cost involved with the ‘so called’ extra and repeated activities will be totally or extensively absorbed by these savings.

The repeated execution of test and deployment activities can be seen as extra work, as are the repeated interviews with business people per iteration or sprint to detail the requirements specifications.  
But this also has an advantage that is not seen. Often the effort that one normally  
spends at the end of a waterfall project, is now spread over the entire project.

Because you perform these activities frequently, you improve them at an early stage, and thus the errors that are solved in that early stage, will ensure it will go flawless in the last iteration, thus ensuring an efficient project acceptance.

**Examples**

As an example I had a deployment to acceptance test environment every iteration. The first 2 iterations showed many problems with differences in the platforms. But the third iteration and all iterations after that, the deployment went smoothly.  
I experienced at a customer site that an IT team had to deal with a supplier who claimed extra costs to make up for the repeated test activities. The costs were even brought to the customer as ‘Agile’ costs. That is not acceptable. Because that supplier forgets that just by already repeatedly testing every iteration, the test effort at the end will be reduced to a minimum, compared to waterfall. These suppliers assume that this test effort at the end of an agile project will be the same. At that point their conclusion that all other test effort will be extra seems logical, but has to be refuted..
