**Resistance against Agile**  
In my work as software process improvement consultant I have come across many organizations transforming from classic waterfall to agile development already.  
What keeps striking me is the (initial) resistance of management and project managers against adapting this new and more efficient way of working, because they believe they have less control. But the opposite is true, as I would like to explain in this article.

**Mitigating financial risk with fixed prices**  
Many managers in IT only have an MBA background were they learn finance, marketing, HR, sales, general management. So what do they do when they have no knowledge of, or no control over their primary process? They mitigate financial risk by demanding fixed prices from suppliers.  
But this usually results in huge cost for IT projects. When the supplier is topping up the estimations with a risk factor, the IT demand project manager is topping up the estimation with his risk factor to cover his ass. And finally there’s usually also a Business project manager topping up the estimation to cover his. This is control of course, but is it what you would want?

**Fake security**  
Managers are also provided with a fake security, when estimations, leading to the described inflation of project cost, are based on only big piles of paper. Big piles of paper being: Global and Detailed designs, because that is what the supplier demands, when he is asked to provide a fixed price.  
Fast changing business environments, and lack of quality requirements will usually lead to changing scope, which provides the supplier with a reason to ask even more money.  
Not to mention the cost for adapting the piles of paper produced upfront.

**Estimations based on only global requirements**  
Most suppliers are used to base their estimations on designs, not on requirements. So we have spoiled the suppliers with this, because it is deadly easy to make expert estimations based on detailed designs.

**Challenge your supplier**  
So give the suppliers a challenge. Produce good and SMART global requirements and partial detailed requirements. Like in a 80%/20% balance. The 20% detailed requirements (not to be confused with design) being necessary to provide enough basis for development of the first iteration or sprint. This is usually also the functionality containing the biggest technical risk.  
Then ask the supplier to base their estimations on that with a factor of uncertainty. They should be able to do that, but it takes them more effort. That is why they usually complain, when asked to do so. But remember, they are spoiled!  
But with the knowledge of their own product and custom implementation of that product in your customer organization, they should be able to do so.  
They should be able to provide you with for example a 30% tolerance estimation for the 80% globally described requirements, and a 10% tolerance estimation for the 20% of detailed requirements.

**Give IT management another fake security**  
To keep the IT managers happy, why not provide them with another type of fake security? But be transparent about it.  
Communicate openly about the estimations and tolerances. Top up the estimation once with a percentage that will cover for most of the uncertainty, and make that your budget.

**Mitigating financial risk: the agile solution**  
So how does the IT manager gain more control, when not necessarily demanding fixed prices? This seems extremely scary to most managers. But if they want cut on personnel and cost for their CIO, and become friends with the business, they’d better try.

A number of factors can provide the manager with the control needed, without having to ask for fixed prices:  
*1. Directing the iterations or sprints as IT demand*  
Turn your demand IT Project manager into a Scrum master or Iteration Lead, or have your IT Project manager hire one to direct the team (including supplier). In this last case he manages the environment (IT Management, Business Project manager, Stakeholders, Administration, Steering Committee). This means he can not do 10 projects at the same time any more, and it will be a paradigm shift for the project manager involved. He will go from one progress meeting a week with the supplier per project (management by exception), to having to make plans, iteration plans, prepare and facilitate risk sessions, prepare and facilitate assessments or retrospectives. This takes a lot of discipline and extra time per project. But as demand, you are in control of the iterative process, and that is what will give you the ROI on this.

*2. Working software instead of piles of paper at the end of second stage*  
At the moment you get your detailed designs in the classical approach, the iterative project will have delivered working software already, mitigating the biggest technical risk. Based on a detailed design, you can not decide that you will have problems and want to stop the project. But based on the problems that will have risen from developing the most challenging parts of the solution in the agile way at that same point in time, you will be provided with the proof to either proceed with the project or stop it, at the same (or even less) amount of budget burned.

*3. Working and tested software demonstrating pieces of Business Value every sprint or iteration, faster Time to Market*  
With the backlog of requirements representing pieces of Business Value, and developing the requirements in order of Business priority, the biggest Business Value will be delivered first. Demonstrating the working software at the end of each iteration or sprint, will provide the Business with the means to redirect and re prioritize their requirements. This will provide the Business with more control, and the ability to have a faster time to market with the right solution. This will also contribute to a more agile business.

*4. short cycles reduce waist of time*  
Give a team in a classical project budget for half a year to produce something, they will use this budget. A lot of slack will be wasted in this period.  
A team having to reach certain objectives in a short period of time, will have no time to create and use up slack. On the contrary, if they finish their job, and have time left, they are supposed to help other team members to also reach the iteration or sprint objectives. Lead time at the beginning is filled with bug fixing, setting up environments, preparing tests. While slack at the end of the iteration or sprint is used to do retest, rework and redesign, to eliminate the blocking defects, so the software can be demonstrated.

*5. Fixed bandwidth of team hired under time and material, give fixed cost pattern per period*  
With the change of fixing scope in waterfall versus fixing cost and duration in agile, the IT manager will know upfront the amount of cost the project or release will cost. The project stops at the end date. Only possibly leaving could have and would have requirements that have not been developed.  
Using story points to measure velocity of the teams per iteration or sprint, will make the teams predictable very fast, so the chance of loosing could have and would have requirements from your scope can additionally be reduced.

*6. Risk driven way of working gives you the benefit of Böhm’s Law*  
Böhm’s Law says: the later in a project or release you find a defect, the exponentially more expensive the solution will be.  
So actively mitigating the biggest risks or impediments first per iteration or sprint, by developing working software, will deliver the cost reduction that will pay for all the arguments people have against the repetitive nature of agile.  
Some arguments used by people (and suppliers) resisting against agile:

* You have to repeat very much activities over and over, this takes more time, like deployment activities, iteration planning, iteration assessments
* You have to take a lot of time describing the requirements upfront, why not start design right away
* Having to test every iteration will take more test effort
* Having to develop small parts of a solution, and completing the solution the next iteration will increase the chance of introducing new defects in existing code
* Learning a new way of working takes too much effort for this important project, we will not deliver in time

Reading this article now, gives me the impression I wrote a justification for introducing agile, over having proven that you can plan and estimate agile projects. But all the topics described are related to this.

*‘It is better to start with more uncertainty, and start building on increasing your certainty, over not knowing that you are dealing with a fake security’.*
