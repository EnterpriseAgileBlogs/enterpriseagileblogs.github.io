I have already devoted many blogs to organization structures and how they are subject to change due to the worldwide development towards much more customer-oriented working.

It is called tilting from functionally oriented to product-oriented, or from Anglo-Saxon organizing to Rhineland organizing. Because we are no longer in the industrial age, in which Anglo-Saxon organizing worked. We are already almost past the age that followed it, the information age! Organizations must adapt to the development of human consciousness. To retain employees you have to give them ‘purpose’; money is secondary to that.

What has changed most as a result is that an agile organization is never again static according to an org chart. An agile organization is an organization that continuously adapts and improves itself according to circumstances. That means quite a bit of rethinking for all employees. But also for works councils, because is a continuously changing organization not constantly in an organizational change? We need to move towards a completely different way of thinking about governing organizations and how we deal with organizational change. After all, the new status quo is continuous change and improvement!

In this blog I want to go into that ongoing change and improvement of an organization to adapt to circumstances. I do this by linking value streams, or product groups, to the life cycles of a product according to the Boston Matrix.

In an agile organization there is no longer a difference between business and IT. Because, after all, all business is IT-driven due to the entire digitalization of products and product sales. Preferably an organization consists of self-organizing teams of teams oriented on products or value streams. These teams of teams are called ‘Tribes’, ‘Agile Release Trains’ or ‘Grids’. Either an organization adopts the name from a scaling framework, such as ‘Agile Release Train’ (ART) from the Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe), or they come up with their own name for it, such as Grids at ABN-AMRO. But it comes down to the same thing: you have self-organizing teams of multidisciplinary self-organizing teams, with a mix of business and IT in them, that stand for a certain business theme. Below is an example of what that can look like:

![Figure 1: An organization with 3 main products or product groups](/images/blog/boston1.png)

*Figure 1: An organization with 3 main products or product groups*

In an agile organization we only think in the unit of multidisciplinary teams, so no longer in individuals or disciplines that individually deliver intermediate products. A team of teams can contain teams of different kinds. For example multidisciplinary product teams that innovate and manage a business product, or a multidisciplinary staff team with a risk officer, security officer, HR employee, compliance officer, controller etc. It can also contain supporting teams, such as a tool team that manages and configures all supporting tooling for use by the other teams according to the needs of the team of teams they belong to. This means all those teams are oriented on a business product or product group, and that gives focus for everyone, and the so-called ‘purpose motivator’. The fact that together you all stand for that product or product group: ‘That is what we are about!’.

In figure 1 you see an organization with 3 main product groups for which teams of teams are responsible. If this concerned KPN, for example, these could be ‘Television’, ‘Telephony’ and ‘Internet’. Product A is a rising star and is doing well. There is an upward trend in it. 7 teams are responsible for making this product a success. Product B is a ‘cash cow’ according to the Boston Matrix. This product is at the top, and achieves the most revenue for the company. 10 teams run this product. Product C is a new product that they want to put on the market. 1 team applying ‘Lean startup’ principles investigates the viability of this product.

Below you see, following this (simplified) linking of teams of teams to the life cycles of the Boston Matrix, how this can change over time:

![Figure 2: Main products or product groups enter different life cycles](/images/blog/boston2.png)

In figure 2 you see that the number of teams moves along with the life cycle of the product or product group. By shifting this capacity over time across the teams of teams, you also shift the budget that you as a company reserve for innovation and management of that product. In this case more budget in total has been allocated in 2020, because there are 2 more teams than in 2019. On the other hand, we do have 2 products that generate a lot of revenue.

The pattern is clear. Of course there is a lot to say about this. It is only a rough representation of what reality will be. In reality we also have things like front office employees who, for example, have to sell all products, or sales. Where do they sit then? And the degree to which organizations have to act on circumstances also depends on the degree to which their services or products are fairly stable or even rigid.

And you cannot think strictly in pure product or customer value teams; you always also have generic things to think about. See my blog on that: </en/blog/fractal-structure-patterns-when-scaling-agile-enterprise-wide/>

What has been sketched here at macro level, namely teams of teams representing product groups, of course also happens at micro level. Within a team of teams you have product teams that are also individually responsible for a product that is likewise subject to the life cycles of the Boston Matrix. For example, KPN's ‘Telephony’ product group could contain teams representing ‘Fixed telephony’ and ‘Mobile telephony’. You could easily imagine that the product ‘Fixed telephony’ is a ‘DOG’, which thereby makes the size of the ‘Telephony’ team of teams shrink. But if ‘Mobile telephony’ becomes a ‘cash cow’, that product again needs more teams. There you have the balance that portfolio, together with the product managers of those product groups, must seek every quarter.

*Figure 6: stages of agility of organizations (© Reinventing Organizations: A guide to creating organizations inspired by the next stage of human consciousness, by Frederic Laloux)*

In this figure the stages of agility of organizations are visualized. From ‘Command & Control’ (red and yellow, extremely rigid) to the ultimate Rhineland or holacratic organization (teal, very agile), and everything in between.

I see the movement described as an intermediate phase in the possible movement towards even more network-oriented organizations and a far-reaching form of self-organization and mobility. Most organizations where I work (or have worked) are in yellow and/or orange.
