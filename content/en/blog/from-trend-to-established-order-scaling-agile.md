**The trend**

After 50 years of doing IT business the same way, we are finally seeing a turning point.

Due to the pressure of:

1.  the rapidly changing economy (hence the need for a faster ‘Time to Market’);
2.  increasing complexity in IT landscapes (technical debt and interfaces);
3.  the classic governance paradigm no longer fitting the many Scrum teams in IT;

scaling up agile and lean ways of working enterprise-wide has gone from a trend to a fact. If companies do not embark on this, it will be the death of them, if they do not already die from the lean start-ups that can quickly overtake them.

Companies and products are becoming more and more ‘IT driven’. And to name another such big trend, after that even more ‘Data driven’.

Organizations will have to become increasingly agile if they want to respond to all the changes. Not only in terms of processes and ways of working, but the entire organization will have to be subject to constant change and improvement.

(Continuous organization improvement, see also my blog ‘From Scrum team to Agile Organization part 5’: </en/blog/from-scrum-team-to-agile-organization-part-five/>

**The frameworks**

You now see the scaling frameworks popping up like mushrooms. All of them give a starting point for scaling up the principles of agile, Scrum and lean to the enterprise level. Already well-known frameworks are:

1.  LeSS (Large Scale Scrum) by Craig Larman and Bas Vodde;
2.  NeXuS (Scaled Professional Scrum) by Scrum.org (Ken Schwaber);
3.  Spotify, which is actually not a framework, but a reflection of the Spotify organization at a given moment;
4.  SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework) by Dean Leffingwell.

In my opinion all these frameworks have 80% overlap. All of them are based on agile and lean principles. And all of them scale up Scrum ways of working by making a hierarchy of it. Differences lie in naming, details, and the degree of visualization of possible hierarchies. Commercial drivers polarize these models as if they were totally different.

All of them build a hierarchy of the Scrum way of working; only SAFe already visualizes an example hierarchy.

All of them form a team of teams in order to jointly realize integrated solutions that are domain-oriented. All of them call those teams of teams something different.

All of them use Scrum as the basic way of working.

All frameworks are based on the same principles, which say something about an attitude and behaviour that is necessary to be able to work this way, let alone to scale up.

**Interpretation of the term scaling**

With NeXuS, the term scaling agile means executing multi-team projects, i.e. scaling up from working with Scrum in 1 team to working integrated with Scrum with multiple teams.

I myself see scaling more as the trend that an agile way of working is gradually applied more and more across the entire organization, including the business and adapting the organization structure for it. That makes it an equivalent of the term ‘Enterprise agility’. As the Scaled Agile Framework also covers.

So actually the question is more: do you want to limit agile working to IT with multiple teams working together in an agile way, or do you want to extend agile to the entire organization?

Whichever interpretation you give it (and I am more of a generalist, so it does not matter that much to me), an organization that starts on this should be aware of the difference, and set itself an agile ambition.

**Snags, remarks, pros and cons**

There are of course certainly differences and remarks to be made, too many to mention. I will explain a few:

- SAFe is the only one that also offers a solution to the governance issue ('Lean portfolio management' and 'Lean budgeting'), which in practice is difficult to implement due to years of ingrained financial control processes. The other frameworks do not have this.
- Because SAFe already shows a possible hierarchy (of backlogs), there is the danger that it is implemented dogmatically by a ‘blue’ organization and projected as organizational layers of the existing organization. Implementation experts must be very alert to this. We may still remember that this also happened with RUP back then, and that is why people are so allergic to this (far too much already prescribed, and organizations then fail to make it smaller or tailor it to their situation). Worst of all is that Dean Leffingwell also contributed to RUP back then.
- Version 4.0 of SAFe shows an extra hierarchy, which was already in 3.0 but was not visualized. Because of this the purists say that SAFe has only become bigger and more prescriptive. A good consultant, however, sees right through this; an average employee or manager without agile experience does not.
- NeXuS thus only talks about multi-team projects and how to solve them, and applies Scrum almost elementarily fractally. See the following (fractal) figure I made for it.

- LeSS and NeXuS both have a minimal model. And they specifically propagate keeping everything minimal and as flat as possible. Keep the same role names, regardless of the level in the hierarchy, keep the same event names, etc. So these frameworks require an organization to build the hierarchy itself, starting from the existing concepts of Scrum. This already demands a great deal of creativity and knowledge and experience with agile and Scrum from an organization, and that is often not there. Then one has to lean on the advisors. Conversely, as explained for SAFe, the danger is that one wants to implement an already visualized hierarchy 1 on 1. Here too, leaning on good advice is necessary.

**Way of implementation**

Depending on the ambition and willingness to change of an organization, you can implement more as a ‘big bang’, or step by step and in phases. The Spotify model is an example of ‘big bang’. You start by putting the structure in place, and then you begin. This is very disruptive on the one hand; on the other hand, it does strengthen adapting to the new way of working and letting go of the old. Craig Larman of LeSS also says: first rigorously put the new structure in place, and then begin. With companies that do not immediately want to talk about reorganization or restructuring, this is more difficult. Should we not help them then? If I did not, I would have no work. Whether you implement with a ‘big bang’, or step by step, or department by department, there is only one approach that works here, and that is implementing agile ways of working in an agile way. Implementing agile frameworks is not a goal in itself, but merely a means for organizations to be able to cope with the economic changes mentioned at the beginning.

**Established order**

Why from trend to established order? Once again, I believe that large companies that do not pursue agility in order to cope with the rapidly changing economy will not survive. Government agencies aside. Scaling agile, or enterprise agility, will therefore become the established order. Established order may again smell too much of a status quo, but what I mean by it is that organizations must by default engage in continuous organizational improvement/change, and that is by definition not a status quo!
