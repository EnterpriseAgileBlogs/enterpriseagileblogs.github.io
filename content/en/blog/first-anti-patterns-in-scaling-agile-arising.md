![chain ant pattern](/images/blog/chain-ant-pattern.jpg)

In any implementation of a methodology or framework appliance of anti patterns always occur. It is a standard phenomenon when people project their current way of working onto the new way of working. Anti-Patterns are peoples own interpretations of a methodology or framework (usually after a first course), that actually are contradictory to the intention, and thus do not produce the desired outcome as intended by the framework.  
Have a google on anti-patterns in Scrum, of which there are many. Some examples:  
• A team only executing a daily stand-up and then say ‘we apply Scrum!’  (the easiest to apply and then think you’re already there, complacency);  
• Teams saying that a User Story can always move to the next sprint if they do not reach their sprint goals, “because that’s agile”. (This team fails to improve their planning process in order to reach their sprint goals in the future and achieve a stable velocity, and thus a good predictability);  
• Developing a user story in three sprints (Water-falling in sprints);  
• Teams carrying out a retrospective but do nothing with the results;  
• Etc.

In my work as a management coach for major transitions I already bumped into some anti-patterns in scaling agile across an organization. I have particular experience in the application of Scaled Agile Framework.

I will discuss three major anti-patterns that are beginning to manifest themselves:  
1. Plotting the existing organizational structure onto the layers of SAFe;  
2. Renaming existing programs and projects to value streams and release trains.  
3. Management lacking to allocate time for teams for continuous product and process quality improvement

**Plotting the existing organizational structure onto the layers of SAFe**

The SAFe critics are bound to get right on this point. What we see is that large organizations identify their structure with the “big picture” of the Scaled Agile Framework. This with the assumption that they do not need to reorganize, no people need to be re-educated, or let go because of “sufficient” roles in SAFe.  
I encounter many organizations (In Netherlands) with this stratification:  
1. Business  
2. Information Management  
3. IT  
4. Operations

What you see is that people will ‘plot’ the ‘SAFe big picture’ to this as follows:  
1. Business = Portfolio Layer  
2. Information Management = Program Layer  
3. IT = Team Layer  
4. Operations = ‘Fun for when we also want to do something with DevOps’..;-)

The result is that the high walls between existing departments remain undiminished. Every employee is plotting their existing job profiles onto the SAFe layers and roles.  
What we want to see however is that the organizations do not look at the structure anymore, or, at least try to flatten the organization as much as possible. So business close to IT. This means you have multi-disciplinary teams put together from resources of all departments, regardless. Department boundaries no longer matter. With teams of teams we want to focus on frequent delivery of business value for business products. These teams of teams are referred to as Agile Release Trains, Tribes, Nexus’, or product-groups, whichever scaling framework you apply. But significant in this is that management puts the department interest aside, and therefore the sub-optimal goals of a department. They should be replaced by company interest, or even better, the interest of the business product where this team of teams  stands for.  
Plotting job profiles onto roles in SAFe (just as well read Spotify or LeSS), is meaningless because there is no one to one translation. A project manager who previously had a lot of focus on the requirements in his / her project scope, can be a good Product Manager or Product Owner. But did he / she focus more on process, planning, organization and facilitation, then he / she is probably a better Scrum Master or Release Train Engineer.

So what should we look at? To the skills and competencies of the employees. So, having employees apply for a new role, based on their skills and competencies might be a solution.  
But this is difficult in the context of work councils, which should be taken into account. And here again you see where you’re dealing with when you go scaling an agile way of working across the company: a complete reorganization! And that means the commitment of top management to run it as such.  
Some organizations opt for a more gradual introduction, I call it evolutionary. Others go for a big bang approach, more revolutionary. In the evolutionary approach the dangers mentioned before are more likely to occur than when going for revolutionary. However revolutionary also has its own drawbacks. Reorganizing remains difficult!

**Renaming existing programs and projects to value streams and release trains**

Those who have done a SAFe course know that you have to structure your organization into so-called ‘Value streams’. These value streams are then staffed with the teams of teams mentioned earlier. This correlates with the paradigm shift: “from: bringing employees to the work (temporary project teams put together for a project), to: bring the work to fixed product teams (continuous flow of customer requirements are incorporated into continuous product releases by fixed product teams). See my blog about paradigm shifts (In Dutch, still to be translated): [http://www.cibit.nl/nl/nieuws/blogs/paradigmaverschuivingen-bij-scaling-agile](http://www.cibit.nl/nl/nieuws/blogs/paradigmaverschuivingen-bij-scaling-agile/)

Because top management almost never realizes these consequences from day one, nor have given commitment for this new governance model (which it is), you will see many hybrid situations arise. This is partly because the entire agile movement has mostly risen bottom-up from within IT.

(You can put Waterfall next to RUP and Scrum if you want, but not SAFe. SAFe you have to compare to the project matrix governance model)

As long as it remained in IT, doing projects (in the old governance model) with Scrum, was not a problem. But when scaling and flipping the organization from functional to business oriented silos, decisions on board level are needed to guide the organization in the right direction. With the evolutionary approach, you will see a hybrid situation arise. I always advocate that you should start with one domain as a pilot, and then expand. That is implicitly creating a hybrid situation.  
By hybrid I mean an organization that is partially already organized in fixed business silos with the new (SAFe) governance (whether virtual (independent of existing organizational structure) or literally (organizational structure adapted to business silo’s)), and partially still domains working in the old paradigm: temporary programs and projects in the classical project matrix governance.

If top management, however, is not aware of this hybrid situation, then a sub optimal situation arises in which you can not determine the programs, projects from the value streams and release trains, let alone the org chart. Two governance models are used simultaneously, creating even more overhead. If this expectation management (along with benefit management) is not done properly, it will result in management rejecting agile, because it does not deliver what it promises.

Another phenomenon arising here is that organizations rename existing temporary programs to a release train, and temporary projects to teams. Then they think they are done (complacency again), but in fact you maintain the same complexity of a project matrix organization with temporary projects. And that is what we want to get rid of.

The term ‘Program’ as in the program layer in the SAFe ‘Big picture’, is confusing here, and not helping a lot. This makes many people think (especially in Europe) that we are talking about programs in terms of MSP (Managing Successful Programs), and Prince2 behind that.

A temporary program or project in the new situation, becomes an **Epic**. A customer requirement, or even a rationalization program as Architectural Epic, which must be picked up by one or more fixed, business-oriented release trains, chopped into pieces, and released into production. This is a **radical** new way of managing IT. I can not emphasize it enough.

Top management has to deal consciously with this change, and gradually introduce domain after domain. This top-down control of the change is necessary, on top of the bottom-up implementation of a new way of working, different behavior and applying best practices and continuous improvement.

**Management lacking to allocate time for teams for continuous product and process quality improvement**

This is not a new anti-pattern. When utilizing Scrum this also happens already everywhere I visit. It is very striking, you would think that if one (and who is ‘one’) embraces the agile and lean principles (which you do when embracing Scrum and SAFe), you also embrace the principle of continuous improvement. But this is lacking on a large scale (see also the exemplary anti patterns on Scrum level at the beginning of this blog).  
As a reminder, the 4th pillar of the Lean Temple says: “Relentless improvement”.  Principle No. 12 of the Manifesto for Software Development says: “At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to Become more effective, then tunes and adjusts its behavior accordingly”.

Concretely this means to me in practice: Each team will receive 10% of the available time per sprint for process improvement, allocated to them by the Product Owner. That 10% is a guideline, but the point is that there is something agreed. In SAFe I go further, to be able to improve the team of teams we have the so-called IP sprint, and within this sprint, the Inspect and Adapt session. IP stands for innovation and planning sprint. This entire sprint is dedicated to improving product and process quality. So cross team improvements to optimize the team of teams.  
If I count the allocations for process improvement together, then I come to a (directive) 20% overall allocation for a team of teams to spend structurally on product and process quality improvement.

What do I see in practice: one, and this time it is usually management, can not justify allocating time to product and process quality. One is tied to KPIs as hours per function point (“Yikes! The number of hours per function point will increase if we do this!). Or, it is a governance issue. Only functional business Epics or Architectural Epics have a budget. Previously, the project had a budget, and now the Epics. And beyond the budget of the Epics in the backlogs, there is no budget.  
So I regularly defend as a management consultant why time spent on product and process quality in the long term will show an increase in productivity! And that refusing to (transparently) allocate the suggested time for this results in a status quo, which will show a reduction in productivity on the long term.  
Here you see again that the ingrained governance paradigms are difficult for management to let go or change, for many reasons. This is all the more reason to bring the consequences of an initiative for scaling agile under the attention of top management. And I mean training and support of top management. Without this basis, agile and lean principles will be ignored from day one,  while management thinks and says they apply agile principles, when choosing for scaling, or even worse, for a specific scaling framework
