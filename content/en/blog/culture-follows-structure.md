# Business and IT in one release train? It is finally explicit in SAFe 5.0!

## Introduction

I am very happy with the most recent version of the Scaled Agile Framework. This 5.0 version will probably go live in January 2020. This version now explicitly says what was intended from the beginning, but was never made explicit: a value stream contains all employees, regardless of department, who together can realize and evolve the business solutions for that value stream.

However, I have proposed the concept of including the business and staff departments in release trains in organizations more often, and I also touch on it in my blog: ‘Job classification systems? Demolish them!’: [/en/blog/job-classification-systems-demolish-them/](/en/blog/job-classification-systems-demolish-them/)

Not that organizations are keen on it. Managers of uni-disciplinary silos such as staff departments prefer to have their employees together on 1 floor. But staff departments should go the same way as all other disciplines that previously belonged to a functional silo, such as the test department. Testers are now included in multidisciplinary teams, and safeguard their expertise and knowledge sharing in communities. So that is how it should go with the staff departments too.

With business departments that may be different. They are often already organized around products or product lines. Think of the life insurance department at an insurance company.

How do I envision this, and how does SAFe 5.0 look at it? I try to make this clear using my own visualizations.

## The classic functional silos

![silo.png](/images/blog/silo.png)

This is how many existing organizations still look. Over time all specializations have become pillarized into functional silos that are mostly uni-disciplinary and pursue their own (departmental) goals rather than the goals of the products the company delivers. In this very simplified example I assume for a moment that the business departments are already organized into distinct product groups.

## Overlay of Release Trains

![silo2.png](/images/blog/silo2.png)

The Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe) lays Agile Release Trains (ARTs, or self-organizing teams of teams) over the value streams (here referred to as product groups).

I will leave the Release Train mentioned here as the 4<sup>th</sup> out of consideration for now. How you should interpret it I have already explained extensively in this blog: ‘Fractal structure patterns when scaling agile enterprise-wide’: [/en/blog/fractal-structure-patterns-when-scaling-agile-enterprise-wide/](/en/blog/fractal-structure-patterns-when-scaling-agile-enterprise-wide/)

## Different kinds of teams

The transition that both business and staff departments need to make is now as follows:

1.  The department starts organizing and working agile itself
2.  The department splits up and divides itself over:
    1.  The value streams

    <!-- -->

    1.  A corporate staff or portfolio team to be formed
3.  The individual employees focus on working in a multidisciplinary team aimed at value creation, specialize further in agile working and safeguard their expertise in communities (for example an HR community)

## Abolishing the existing structure

SAFe 5.0 now states that you can maintain the existing hierarchy, and achieve the agility you want with the informal network on top of it (the visualized overlay).

On this point I do not agree with the authors of SAFe 5.0. It should not stop here. Maintaining the existing hierarchy only leads to even more ineffectiveness, sub-optimization, entanglement of interests, and so on. Departments keep their own ‘departmental plans’, ‘departmental meetings’, ‘departmental targets’, ‘departmental reports’ etc. All while everyone in the organization should be focusing on the product life cycle, and not on sub-optimal departmental goals.

Only then can you become a truly agile organization, in my opinion. For me, agility is therefore based above all on an agile structure, as many of my blogs show. Of course you also need an agile IT landscape (loosely coupled), and an agile strategy. But an agile structure follows from an agile strategy, and, as the title says: Culture follows structure.

For a good visualization of the agile structure, see also my blog: ‘The agile organization analogous to the Boston Matrix’: [/en/blog/the-agile-organization-analogous-to-the-boston-matrix/](/en/blog/the-agile-organization-analogous-to-the-boston-matrix/)

So: away with that rigid ERP system that does not allow you to easily move employees, or set up departments, or close down departments. And the request for advice to the works council and FZ should be: ‘As an organization we want to become agile, and to that end drastically simplify changing the organization structure and make all requests for advice needed for this redundant in the future’.

## Epilogue

Of course you want to make an agile organization as flat as possible, with single teams that are totally responsible for a business product. Practice makes that difficult. Where it is possible, you should certainly do it, and ‘de-scale’ rather than ‘scale’. The examples I use here are only to illustrate main principles, and are mainly applicable to large organizations. In detail every situation is different; every organization is different in size, with different goals. Tailoring whichever framework you use as an aid to becoming agile is therefore always necessary.

In my experience (I have been using the Scaled Agile Framework for 7 years to tilt organizations from functional to product-oriented structures) organizations can benefit enormously in several respects if you apply this well and adequately. Practice, however, always remains unruly.
