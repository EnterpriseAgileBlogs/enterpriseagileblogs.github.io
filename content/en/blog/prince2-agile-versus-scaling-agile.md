In this blog a short reflection and my personal opinion on Prince2 Agile, set against the rise of the phenomenon of scaling agile, and more specifically the Scaled Agile Framework.

The rise of agile working, which can no longer be stopped, has led the organization that maintains Prince2 (AXELOS) to recently come out with Prince2 Agile.

It combines the classic way of project management with agile as a body of thought, and the many agile frameworks out there.

**Introduction to Prince2 Agile**

Below are some lists of things Prince2 Agile uses as its starting points:

Prince2 Agile covers the following themes:

- Progress
- Business Case
- Organization
- Quality
- Plans
- Risk
- Change

It applies the following principles:

- Continued business justification
- Learning from experience
- Defined roles and responsibilities
- Managing by stage
- Management by exception
- Focus on products
- Tailoring to suit the project environment

Prince2 Agile says about Agile: ‘Prince2 Agile regards Agile as a ‘family of concepts, behaviours, frameworks and techniques’.

Under concepts they list:

- Prioritizing
- Iterative
- Incremental
- Inspect & Adapt
- Kaizen

Under behaviours they list:

- Transparency
- Collaboration
- Communication
- Self-organization
- Exploration

Under frameworks they list:

- Scrum
- Kanban
- DSDM
- SAFe
- Lean
- Lean start-up

Under techniques they list:

- User story
- Time box
- Burn chart
- Retrospective

**Reflection**

I have been a project manager myself, have done Prince2 trainings and have run projects according to Prince2. I think it is good that Prince2 Agile is based on agile principles and concepts, and responds to a more flexible execution.

However, I find it rather simplistic to say we apply Prince2 ‘as is’, and for delivery you can stick any agile framework underneath it. Then they have not yet understood that, for example, with agile fixed-scope thinking is abandoned, and that the SAFe framework assumes that you no longer do projects. They do say that you can do a greenfield development of a product with Prince2 Agile, and when it is finished hand it over to the standing SAFe organization, which will carry out further innovation and maintenance on the product as ‘business as usual’. I am not in favour of that last part either.

So when I look at it from my current practice with the, also lasting, trend of Scaling Agile, I have to make the following remarks:

- A given in the market is that the classic governance of stand-alone projects of a temporary nature, with steering committees and classic KPIs such as spend versus budget, no longer works when you start running all projects agile at large scale, which is why scaling agile is booming so much;
- Working in projects (they do say fixed teams, but for the duration of the project; after that such a team disintegrates again? or do you let them move into that maintenance train?) requires double (project) bookkeeping. And that brings an enormous overhead with it;
- SAFe assumes fixed teams within domains (value streams), which (for IT-driven companies) realize releases for business products or business processes for an indefinite period;
- These fixed teams, organized in organizational units with a fixed cost centre, you budget per six months; you simply take the salary costs as your basis, no more double project bookkeeping;
- If within a domain there is a need for an entirely new product, such as developing an app, you can also choose to immediately embed a new team in the fixed SAFe structure to do the greenfield development of the app. Compose that train with teams with a core of your own employees, supplemented by suppliers if needed;
- In this way you move the resourcing along like a mammoth tanker with the growth or drying up of the backlogs in the flow of requirements for a release train;
- See the scope of such an app project as an Epic that enters a release train, which for the greenfield development sets up an extra team that is a mix of people from existing teams with domain knowledge and new people;
- This prevents that double bookkeeping, and the withdrawal of resources from fixed release trains, which in turn affects their productivity.

**My final conclusions, again seen through the lens of scaling agile:**

- For organizations that are somewhat less IT-driven (which is becoming increasingly rare) and organizations that have very little maintenance on their IT landscape (which is also rare because everyone has a lot of technical debt in their IT landscapes), I would recommend stand-alone projects, and therefore Prince2 Agile.
- For non-IT projects such as organizing a dance festival: Prince2 Agile is very suitable!
- For intensively IT-driven organizations I recommend, if they want to go agile, and if they want to stay competitive or become more agile, to embrace SAFe and to structure the organization from business to infra along value streams. That means NO more projects (and therefore no Prince2 Agile, because SAFe and projects on top of that, or in parallel, those clash). Within those value streams, release trains run with multidisciplinary teams, with the following advantages:
  - Multidisciplinary teams are owners (and also feel that way) of the product (intrinsic motivation);
  - Multidisciplinary teams carry out innovation and maintenance in 1 team (so also with greenfield development: ‘shared code ownership’ from the first line of code);
  - Multidisciplinary teams build up domain knowledge and retain it;
  - Multidisciplinary teams build up a frame of reference for a new form of predictability that in the long run makes intensive up-front planning and budgeting efforts redundant;
  - No more start-stop waiting times for starting and stopping projects;
  - No more steering committees, and therefore expensive reports that do not contribute to business value
  - No more exception reports (governance is based on business value and productivity, no longer on costs, because those are fixed);
  - No more ‘Project Cost Accounting’;
  - Many meeting structures that become completely redundant;
  - Implicit reduction of technical debt;
  - Implicit continuous process improvement;
  - Moving the resourcing of release trains along on a six-monthly rhythm (mammoth tanker) in line with the product life cycle.
